# Should You Trade Options? | Options Trading for Beginners Ep8

## Summary

This episode concludes the Outlier Options Trading Boot Camp series by addressing the fundamental question: should you trade options at all? Eric and guest CG work through the decision framework, examining the complexity, time investment, and skill requirements against realistic return expectations. The discussion emphasizes that outperformance doesn't require heroic returns—managing drawdowns and participating in upside can beat the market—and that options trading is viable for disciplined traders with proper position sizing and thesis-driven entry/exit rules.

## Key takeaways

### Dated market read (2026-08-08)

- **Volatility term structure varies by security:** Palantir shows relatively flat IV across expirations (54.8% to 55.7%), while Home Depot exhibits a spike at the front month (20% at 7 DTE) due to upcoming earnings, then normalizes (37%, 35%, 32%, 30%) [25:32]–[26:50]. This signals market expectations for near-term events.
- **Options market prices in known catalysts:** The volatility surface is a predictive tool; earnings moves, geopolitical events, and leadership changes are reflected in IV skew before they occur [26:50]–[28:24].

### Evergreen mechanics

- **Implied volatility is annualized; scale it to your timeframe:** A 42% 30-day IV does not mean 42% move over 30 days. Use the formula: `spot price × IV × √(DTE / 365)` to convert to expected move for your duration [16:16]–[21:51].
- **One standard deviation move captures ~68% of outcomes:** IV represents the market's expectation of a one-sigma move; understanding the normal distribution is foundational to options pricing [13:10]–[14:28].
- **IV skew and volatility smile reflect risk compensation:** Out-of-the-money puts trade at higher IV (put skew) because sellers demand premium for tail risk; OTM calls trade higher due to unlimited upside and growth factors [33:20]–[35:45].
- **High IV rank ≠ automatic sell signal:** Just because IV percentile is above 90 doesn't mean you should sell premium. You must verify that implied volatility exceeds realized volatility (positive risk premium) [38:04]–[39:36].
- **Position sizing starts with thesis, not account size:** Define where you are theoretically wrong (chart-based, not percentage-based), then size the position to fit your portfolio risk limit. If the position doesn't fit, don't trade it [29:19]–[35:49].
- **Probability of touch ≠ probability of expiration:** A short call may have 42% probability of being touched but only 20% probability of expiring ITM; use spreads to avoid whipsaw stops [32:02]–[33:26].
- **Time decay is a two-edged sword:** Long options lose value as expiration approaches; model your profit target against the time available and cross-check with the options market's probability of touch [37:27]–[40:14].
- **Edge exists in five buckets:** risk premia (timeless), inefficiencies (hard for retail), structural flows, informational gaps, and liquidity provision. Identify which bucket your strategy occupies [50:28]–[58:56].
- **Outperformance doesn't require beating the market every year:** Eric underperformed for his first seven years; the key is limiting drawdowns (his largest was 34%, requiring 52% recovery) and participating in upside [18:11]–[19:45].
- **Small accounts have edges large money can't access:** Leverage, liquidity, and structural inefficiencies in smaller securities and shorter expirations are unavailable to institutional portfolios [21:02]–[21:35].
- **Develop nine core trader competencies:** educator, researcher, data analyst, strategy designer, trader, portfolio manager, risk manager, psychologist, and program manager. Most traders have significant gaps; systematic improvement is required [58:56]–[01:07:13].
