# Should You Start Trading Options? | The Options Trench

## Summary

Eric and Chris explore whether retail traders should attempt active trading, examining research on trader failure rates, the psychological and skill requirements for success, and the conditions under which trading might be a viable pursuit. They argue that while most people should not trade, those with specific personality traits, risk tolerance, and commitment to deliberate practice may find value in the attempt—particularly if they maintain a W2 job as a safety net and approach the endeavor with eyes wide open.

### Evergreen mechanics

- **Research consensus on trader failure** [01:16–02:47]: Most published research focuses on day traders specifically; failure rates are high, though exact percentages (98%, 85%) are often unsourced. The scarcity of rigorous data itself is telling.

- **Default position: don't trade** [03:16–05:21]: For most people, dollar-cost averaging into broad index funds (VTI) is the superior strategy. Trading requires a high hurdle to justify the time and risk. However, low probability ≠ impossible; the question is whether you "check enough boxes" to warrant attempting it.

- **Trading as a skill, not a lifestyle choice** [05:21–06:55]: Attempting trading and finding it's not for you is not a failure or wasted time if pursued with eyes wide open. The key is serious commitment; casual dabbling is different from genuine effort.

- **Constrained resources and opportunity cost** [07:14–08:14]: The argument "you'd be better off focusing on your job" assumes you can't do both and ignores that "if not now, when?" for risky pursuits. Some people are drawn to trading regardless; the question becomes how to allocate effort wisely.

- **The allure of independence** [10:09–12:01]: Trading offers a unique payoff beyond money: independence, no clients, no politics, no need to answer to anyone. This attracts certain personality types (disagreeable, skeptical, entrepreneurial) who may be "unemployable" in traditional settings. For such people, discouraging trading is futile; better to teach them what's required.

- **W2 job as optimal starting position** [16:00–16:45]: Having a W2 job provides a safety net while learning. You can afford to pay "tuition" (losses) without going into debt. If trading doesn't work out, you return to work. This is one of the best possible positions to attempt trading.

- **Capital must be abstract** [17:12–18:18]: Trading capital cannot be viewed as rent money or food for your kids. You must abstract it like poker chips. When starting out, expect to lose money as part of learning. Better to pay tuition while employed than to risk financial ruin.

- **Utility function beyond benchmarks** [19:31–20:18]: Retail traders often fixate on "beating the S&P 500," but this is myopic. The real question is: does the bundle of money, work satisfaction, skill development, and lifestyle fit your actual goals? Different imperatives drive different people.

- **Primary reason to trade: make money** [20:18–20:55]: If you're actively trading, your goal must be profit. Competing against others who are all trying to make money, you cannot afford to have a different objective. This is not about being "right"; it's about making money.

- **Independence and self-reliance** [25:14–27:44]: Trading is one avenue to develop a skill nobody can take away from you. Your skill is your "put"—your worst-case strike. Wealth is when your worst-case scenario (having to fend for yourself) still allows you to maintain your lifestyle. This applies to any skill, but trading is uniquely accessible.

- **Defense in depth** [29:31–31:39]: Don't rely solely on trading. Stack multiple income streams and skills. If incapacitated, you need alternatives. Trading is accessible and doesn't require full-time presence, but it's not a complete solution to self-reliance.

- **Benchmarking for active traders** [32:29–34:05]: If trading for a living, the benchmark is not the S&P 500 but whether the edge available in your market, combined with your share of that edge, generates a livable income. Evaluate the entire job holistically: money, work satisfaction, team, opportunity—not just returns.

- **Personality traits required for trading** [36:37–38:29]: You must be ruthlessly honest with yourself, reviewing every trade without bias (not "I was a genius when right, unlucky when wrong"). Confidence must be calibrated—neither irrationally overconfident nor paralyzed by doubt. This balance is delicate and develops over time, not innately.

- **Earned confidence over time** [40:52–45:45]: Confidence cannot exist at the beginning; it develops through experience and feedback. Mock trading and paper trading are essential. Over time, you develop pattern recognition and "earned confidence"—not perfect, but grounded in repeated exposure. Some traders are naturally action-oriented (learn by doing); others are paralyzed by analysis. Both can be coached toward a middle ground.

- **Paper trading is essential practice** [47:14–48:27]: Paper trading is not "real" and thus often dismissed, but this logic is absurd. The Marine Corps doesn't train by going to war; professional athletes practice before competing. Paper trading allows you to build pattern recognition and calibrate confidence without capital risk. Commit to at least one year of paper trading.

- **Failure rate and serious attempts** [48:54–52:13]: Of traders who fail, very few are actually trying hard. Most people don't try hard at anything. The base rate of serious attempts is low. Additionally, trading requires many unglamorous, blocking-and-tackling tasks (record-keeping, analysis, discipline) that most people won't do. The "90% of restaurants fail" stat has the same issue: many owners lack business skills, not cooking skills.

- **The elephant problem** [52:13–54:52]: When starting, don't look at the entire mountain; you'll quit before starting. Instead, take one step at a time. Expect gradual progress. Look back after 1,000 hours of deliberate practice and you'll be amazed at the distance traveled. You only need to see six inches in front of your face at any moment.

- **First steps for a committed beginner** [55:40–01:00:24]: (1) Find a guide (educator, content creator) you trust; (2) consume their material and related high-quality content; (3) paper trade while logging observations without making premature inferences; (4) maintain a learning mindset; (5) don't expect a tangible outcome after 1,000 hours—the outcome is invisible growth and changed thinking. Signal-to-noise is very low; appreciate this from the start.
