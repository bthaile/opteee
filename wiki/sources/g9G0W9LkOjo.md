# The (Not) So Efficient Market Hypothesis?

## Summary

This episode deconstructs the efficient market hypothesis (EMH), clarifying common misconceptions and exploring what actually makes markets efficient or inefficient. Rather than treating EMH as a binary claim that "prices are always right," the discussion frames it as a spectrum with weak, semi-strong, and strong forms—each describing different relationships between information and price. The practical takeaway: markets are efficient enough that easy money doesn't exist, but inefficiencies persist where information is hard to access, liquidity is thin, or hedging is difficult.

## Key takeaways

- **No easy trade principle** [01:46–02:35]: The first instinct when observing a market price should be "why is that price that price?" rather than assuming it's stupid. Low PE stocks are often value traps; high PE stocks reflect genuine growth expectations. The market's pricing reflects reality more often than naive contrarian bets assume.

- **Three forms of EMH** [07:15–08:27]: Weak form (past prices/volume already priced in); semi-strong form (all public information priced in); strong form (public and private information priced in). Most markets exhibit elements across the spectrum; strong form is unrealistic and even its authors treat it as a simplifying assumption, not reality.

- **Grossman-Stiglitz paradox** [09:31–10:28]: If markets were perfectly efficient, there would be no profit incentive to gather information. Markets can only be efficient down to the cost of obtaining information. This explains why AI and faster analysis don't eliminate edge—they just raise the cost floor.

- **Transparency, trust, and hedgeability drive efficiency** [15:12–23:08]: Markets become more efficient when (1) they are transparent and easy to access (S&P 500 vs. real estate); (2) participants trust settlement and execution (LME nickel cancellation destroyed trust); (3) the underlying is hedgeable (derivatives trade near fair value; unhedgeable assets carry risk premiums). Exotic OTC options trade with fat margins because hedging is messy.

- **Active manager underperformance** [27:22–30:49]: SPIVA data shows most active managers underperform benchmarks after costs. This reflects that beating a consensus price requires differentiated information; without it, you pay costs every year and end up below the benchmark. Outperformance is zero-sum; not everyone can be above average.

- **Incentive misalignment in asset management** [32:08–36:33]: Allocators and managers often have weak incentives to outperform. Low-volatility funds can attract flows without taking risk; allocators may prefer low-vol managers to avoid standing out in committee meetings. Once a manager is large, the business model can shift from outperformance to retention.

- **Price informativeness and prediction markets** [37:32–40:42]: Markets aggregate information better than polls because participants have a profit motive. The dinosaur example: a poll might say 50/50 on humans and dinosaurs coexisting, but a prediction market would price it near zero because experts would sell it down. Money-weighted voting (markets) outperforms one-person-one-vote (polls).

- **Speed and information lag** [41:49–44:18]: HFT edge comes from low-latency information and fast execution (the Rothschild carrier-pigeon story). If you're trading on lagged information, you get picked off. Before economic data releases, traders should pull quotes to avoid being hit only when wrong.

- **Finding edge: invert efficiency drivers** [45:15–50:14]: Look for markets where (1) everyone is going the same direction (crowded, overbid/oversold); (2) information is hard to get (small caps); (3) liquidity is thin (higher premium, but less competition); (4) hedging is difficult (natural gas vs. gold arbitrage). Biotech options with wide spreads can offer edge despite high transaction costs if vol moves favorably.

- **The paradox of provable alpha** [52:30–55:43]: Short-term edge (intraday, high sample size) is theoretically provable but practically inaccessible—anyone with an 8 Sharpe ratio wouldn't let you invest. Long-term edge (2–3 years) is likely larger but unprovable by sample size and vulnerable to black-swan events (COVID). The real edge probably lies in longer-dated strategies where few traders are patient enough to compete.

- **Momentum and feedback loops** [57:40–58:16]: The momentum factor (12-month lookback, 1-month hold) illustrates the trade-off between edge size and observability. Shorter-term strategies offer more feedback reps but smaller edges; longer-term strategies offer fatter edges but fewer reps to validate them. The choice depends on whether you prioritize being right or making money.
