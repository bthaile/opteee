# Leopold Aschenbrenner's Probability of Ruin Explained | The Options Trench

## Summary

This episode dissects the near-collapse of Leopold Aschenbrenner's Salp fund, a $225M AI-focused hedge fund that ran 4× leverage on a concentrated long-AI / short-software thesis. Despite being up 80% year-to-date after a 70% drawdown, the fund exemplifies the mathematical inevitability of ruin when leverage combines with concentration. The hosts use Kelly criterion analysis and Monte Carlo simulations to show that such a system faces a 93%+ probability of losing 50% of capital within a single year—a cautionary tale about the gap between analytical brilliance and risk management discipline.

## Key takeaways

- **Concentration + leverage = inevitable ruin** [00:59–01:26] The fund's 100% volatility at 4× leverage creates a cumulative distribution function where the overwhelming majority of outcomes result in losses over a one-year horizon, regardless of Sharpe ratio assumptions.

- **Liquidity evaporation and self-fulfilling bubbles** [24:10–26:43] When a stock runs 10×–30× in months, disciplined sellers exit early; only momentum traders remain. The marginal buyer is weak-handed, creating an air pocket with no real liquidity. Prices between 10× and 30× are "fake prices" created by the buyer's own demand, not price discovery.

- **Leverage conflates direction with timing** [16:11–16:47] Leverage doesn't just amplify returns; it adds a hidden requirement: you must be right on timing *and* direction. Without leverage, the thesis survives; with it, any adverse path—even temporary—triggers a margin call before the eventual payoff.

- **Kelly criterion and fractional Kelly** [37:40–39:41] Full Kelly betting guarantees a 50% drawdown half the time and a 33% chance of losing half your capital before doubling it. Betting more than full Kelly is "completely off sides." Half-Kelly cuts max drawdown by 50% while sacrificing only 25% of expected return—a superior risk-reward profile.

- **Path dependence vs. path independence** [31:38–34:29] Leverage is path-dependent; options (e.g., put spreads, OTM calls with fixed premium) are path-independent. Risk-budgeting via defined-premium strategies allows thesis expression without margin-call risk, though timing still matters for payoff.

- **Luck vs. skill in survival** [41:29–43:11] The fund survived a 70% drawdown and remains up 80% YTD—but only by ~$2B of margin cushion at 4× leverage. Had losses continued, bankruptcy was imminent. Survival feels like luck; retrospective narratives credit genius, but the math shows the outcome was always probabilistically fragile.

- **Amaranth / Brian Hunter parallel** [27:07–29:57] The natural gas spread trade (March–April futures) ran from 50¢ to $2.50 on Hunter's buying, then collapsed back to 50¢ when fundamentals shifted. John Arnold's 50¢ bid revealed the true value; all prices in between were artifacts of thin liquidity and the trader's own demand.

- **Hubris and the Leopold & Loeb analogy** [18:44–19:51] Brilliant people often fall prey to overconfidence, believing their intelligence exempts them from basic risk math. The Leopold & Loeb murder case (early 1900s) mirrors this: two geniuses convinced they could commit the "perfect crime" and prove their superiority, only to be undone by careless details.

- **Probability of ruin over 5 years** [35:13–35:39] With a Sharpe of 1.0 or better, a 99%+ probability of total wipeout over five years. Even optimistic assumptions yield near-certain ruin given the leverage and concentration profile.

- **Investor sophistication ≠ risk awareness** [07:52–08:39] Jane Street and other smart backers invested despite seeing the risk profile. The question remains: why do sophisticated investors fund strategies with overwhelming probability of failure, even if returns are spectacular when they don't fail?

## Regime / context

**Date:** 2026-07-31 (one week after Salp fund's 70% drawdown on 2026-07-24).

**Market backdrop:** AI infrastructure stocks (DRAM, EWY, Nebius, SanDisk, Micron, Bloom Energy) had rallied 3×–30× over the prior year before correcting 30–50% in late July 2026. Software ETF (IGV) had experienced a 35% max drawdown over the same period. The fund's long-AI / short-software thesis was mechanically sound until liquidity evaporated and leverage forced capitulation.

**Broader lesson:** This episode is part of a recurring pattern in finance—LTCM (1998), Amaranth (2006), Archegos (2021)—where leverage + concentration + correlation collapse = ruin, regardless of the trader's intelligence or initial thesis quality. The mathematical framework (Kelly, Monte Carlo, probability of ruin) has been known for 75 years; the gap between knowledge and execution remains the central mystery.
